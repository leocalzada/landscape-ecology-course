SLIDE 1Title

"Last step of the ladder: individuals, habitat, populations, communities, and today ecosystems. We stop counting animals and species and ask how much plant growth each piece of the Southwest makes, and why."

SLIDE 2From communities to ecosystems

Figure: sunlight + CO₂ enter the plant (GPP); part leaves as respiration; what stays is NPP.

Ecosystem level = pools (how much is stored) and fluxes (how fast it moves). This week the flux is primary production. Two questions (Turner & Chapin 2005): does landscape pattern explain where land is productive? Do patches feed each other?

"Same landscapes as before, but now we ask how much carbon each piece of land fixes, and why that changes from place to place."

SLIDE 3GPP, NPP and ANPP

NPP = GPP − Ra. GPP = all carbon fixed; Ra = what plants burn to live (~half); NPP = what stays; ANPP = the part above ground we clip and weigh.

"GPP is the paycheck, respiration is the rent, NPP is what's left to spend, and ANPP is the part we can see and weigh."

SLIDE 4NDVI

Figure: x = wavelength, y = share of light bounced back. Leaves (green) absorb red and bounce near-infrared: a big jump. Soil and dry grass: no jump.

NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red). Top = size of the jump; bottom = total brightness, so bright and dark pixels compare.

SLIDE 5Greenness is not production

NPP = ε × fPAR × PAR (Monteith 1972): PAR = usable sunlight; fPAR = share absorbed (rises with NDVI); ε = carbon made per unit of light (falls with drought, cold). Over a year: add up NDVI over the season. Traps: soil background, saturation (> 0.8), green ≠ useful.

"NDVI tells us how green; light-use efficiency and time tell us how much. On Wednesday we check whether green really means production."

SLIDE 6Climate sets the line

ANPP = −34 + 0.6 × PPT (Sala et al. 1988, ~9,500 Great Plains sites, ~90% explained). Slope 0.6 = g of plant per extra mm. Las Cruces 250 mm → 0.6 × 250 = 150 → 150 − 34 = 116 g/m². RUE = 116/250 = 0.46 g per mm.

Space vs time: across places the slope is steep; one place across wet and dry years, gentler (Lauenroth & Sala 1992).

"In the Southwest, if you know the rain you know most of the productivity. Most, not all, and the rest is the landscape."

SLIDE 7The landscape moves points off the line

Figure: grey dots follow the line; blue above (irrigation, river, run-on); orange below (dunes, city, burned forest).

Residual = observed − predicted. Positive = water subsidy (water that did not fall as rain on that pixel). Negative = substrate, pavement, fire, overgrazing.

"Climate draws the line. The landscape decides who sits above it and who sits below it."

SLIDE 8Islands of fertility

Figure: left, grassland: rain soaks in where it falls. Right, shrubland: bare gaps send runoff and blown soil to shrubs.

Schlesinger et al. 1990: resources pile up under shrubs, a feedback that keeps grass out. Ludwig et al. 2005: trigger → transfer → reserve → pulse. Configuration changes function, not only composition.

"In a desert, a plant's water does not only come from the sky. It also comes from its neighbour's bare ground."

SLIDE 9The Jornada

  • 25 km north of NMSU; studied since 1912 (USDA) and 1982 (LTER).
  • 1850s grassland → mesquite dunes, creosote, tarbush today.
  • ANPP clipped every year since 1989 at 15 sites, 5 vegetation types.
  • Huenneke et al. 2002: similar mean ANPP in shrublands, but much patchier. Peters et al. 2004: one shrub trapping soil can cascade to the landscape.

"The best natural laboratory for this topic is 25 km from this room."

SLIDE 10Toolbox

Clip plots (1 m²) → flux towers (~1 km²) → Sentinel-2 10 m, Landsat 30 m, MODIS 250 m → products (MODIS GPP/NPP, RAP NPP 30 m for rangelands) → PRISM for rain. Small pixels or frequent images, rarely both.

"Everything we do this week can be downloaded for any rectangle you draw. The app is on Canvas."

SLIDE 12Phenology

Figure: x = month, y = mean NDVI of that month. Left: rain decides when (San Rafael jumps with the monsoon; Organ Pipe greenest in winter; Flagstaff green all year). Right: water from elsewhere (pecans Apr–Oct; Yuma peaks in spring with winter crops; Phoenix flat).

"In the desert, when you are green matters as much as how green. Production over a year is the area under the curve."

SLIDE 13Fire

Las Conchas, June 2011. Mean NDVI 0.53 (2010) → 0.35 (2013). Change −0.18; −0.18/0.53 = −34%. Same rain, same elevation.

"Climate sets the line, but a single day of fire can drop a forest to the level of a grassland for decades."

SLIDE 1433 years at the Jornada

Figure: green line = mean clipped ANPP of 15 sites; blue bars = rain that year. Droughts 1994 (42) and 2003 (53); wet 2007–2008 (~225 g/m²). Right: playa 179, grassland 131, mesquite 113, creosote 88, tarbush 74.

Time slope: ANPP ≈ 34 + 0.30 × PPT (r = 0.45), half of Sala's 0.6. Summer (Jul–Sep) rain predicts better (r = 0.62).

"Same desert, same plants, fivefold differences between years. On Wednesday we see whether the satellite sees the good and the bad years."

SLIDE 15Wrap-up

  • Ecosystems = pools and fluxes; this week, primary production.
  • NDVI is greenness, not production; check it on the ground.
  • Climate sets the line.
  • Water subsidies, substrate, cities, fire and lateral flows move places off it.
